A student asked me why we use a database instead of just writing to a file.

I opened my mouth to answer and realised that what I had was not an explanation. It was twenty years of never having considered the alternative. I knew the answer the way you know your own address — completely, and without ever having derived it.

That is the moment I started taking tutoring seriously.

## Fluency is not understanding

Experienced developers build a large set of compiled decisions. You do not reason about whether to use a transaction; your hands do it. This is what makes you fast, and it is genuinely valuable.

It also hides the gaps. You can go a decade without noticing that you cannot explain *why* one of those decisions is right, because nothing in the job requires you to. Code review does not ask you. Architecture documents do not ask you. Only a beginner asks you, and most senior developers have arranged their lives so that they rarely talk to beginners.

Every session I teach, somebody asks me a question that I can answer correctly and cannot justify. That is not a comfortable experience. It is the most useful one available to me.

## The good questions are the naive ones

The questions that expose the most are never the advanced ones. They are:

- Why do we write tests if we are going to check it manually anyway?
- What is actually wrong with putting this in a global variable?
- Why is this "bad code"? It works.

Notice that these all have a real answer, and that the real answer is "it depends," and that "it depends" is worthless to someone learning. You have to actually do the work: on what does it depend, and how would you know, and what goes wrong in the case where you get it wrong.

Doing that work out loud, repeatedly, for people who will tell you when it did not land, is a better education in software design than the last five years of my professional work.

## What I changed about how I teach

I stopped explaining, mostly.

My early sessions were me talking while the student watched. They understood everything, in the room, for about forty minutes. Then they went home and could not do it. Understanding an explanation and being able to produce the thing are unrelated skills, and only one of them is what they are paying me for.

Now the student types and I mostly ask questions. It is slower, it is more uncomfortable for both of us, and it is the only thing I have found that transfers.

## Why I moved most of my time into this

I still build — the tennis platform, product work through ParionTech. But tutoring has become the part of the week I would not give up.

Partly because of the effect on my own thinking. Mostly because of a specific, repeatable moment: someone who was stuck for weeks suddenly is not, and you can see it happen. Twenty years of accumulated expensive lessons, and the most efficient thing I have found to do with them is hand them to one person at a time.
